### History and future editorial note

Customer experience existed long before 1970. One verified milestone in this pack is the first retail UPC barcode scan on 26 June 1974 [1]. This is a technology example, not the birth of CX.

Other “picture 1970” scenes are illustrative learning scenarios, not universal historical claims. Present-day scenes are exercises; all 2050 scenes are imagined possibilities, not forecasts. Newer tools do not automatically create greater trust, inclusion or useful service.

The accessibility framing draws on W3C's introduction to web accessibility [2]. The beginner exercises are not a complete accessibility audit or certification.

## Day 1 — See the Person Behind the Transaction

🎯 **DAY 1 OF 7 — THE CX MINDSET SHIFT**

Welcome! Today, look beyond the sale or service task.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes: read, reflect and share.**

### 💭 An order is placed. Is the experience complete?

A customer realises the delivery address is wrong.

The order confirmation says “Thank you.” The customer needs to know whether the address can still be changed—and how.

The transaction succeeded. An important need remains.

**CX connects what an organisation promises with what people actually encounter.**

Customer service is one part of CX. Product information, pricing clarity, checkout, delivery and follow-up can also shape the experience.

### 🕰️ A brief journey: 1970 → 2050

**1970s — A real milestone:** On 26 June 1974, a pack of chewing gum was scanned using a UPC barcode at a supermarket in Troy, Ohio [1]. It is one example of technology changing a customer-facing process.

**Today — Follow the whole experience:** A person may move between a website, a message and a support conversation. Ask whether the steps connect.

**Imagine 2050:** AI assistants could coordinate more routine requests across services. People would still need understandable choices, trustworthy information and a route to accountable help.

The future scene is a possibility, not a prediction.

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“We completed the transaction.” → “Can the customer achieve what they came to do?”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Clear information can help someone avoid a wasted trip, repeated calls or an unsuitable purchase.

Consider people with different access needs, language preferences and levels of digital confidence.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**A good experience may help someone decide not to buy.**

Honest information can reveal that an offer does not fit their need. Respecting that choice matters.

### ✍️ Your 5-minute action

Choose one customer moment. Reply:

- **My customer moment:** ___
- **What the person is trying to do:** ___
- **One difficulty they might face:** ___
- **What a useful result would look like:** ___
- **Why this matters:** ___

**Example:** Change an address before dispatch; unclear eligibility; understand whether the change is possible and receive an accurate next step.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Save your “why” sentence. It is your **Origin Story**. You can label the need **NEED-01**, but the label is optional.

🎉 **Today's win:** You can connect a transaction with a person's purpose.

## Day 2 — Walk the Customer Journey

🔍 **DAY 2 OF 7 — FIND THE MOMENT THAT MATTERS**

Yesterday, you chose a customer need. Today, break the experience into three small steps.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. No diagramming tool required.**

### 💭 The website says “Contact support”

The customer opens chat. Chat asks them to call. The phone agent asks them to return to the website.

Every channel exists. The journey still goes in circles.

A **customer journey** describes the steps a person takes toward a goal. A **touchpoint** is an interaction along that journey, such as a web page or a phone call.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

These are illustrative scenes.

**Picture 1970:** A customer visits a counter and calls later for an update.

**Today:** A person may switch between a store, website, app and conversation.

**Imagine 2050:** An assistant could help carry the next step across channels with appropriate permission. People would still need to understand what happened and retain meaningful choices.

The enduring question: **“Do the steps connect from the customer's perspective?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“Our channels are available.” → “The customer can move forward.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Repeated contacts consume time for customers and staff. Someone on limited connectivity or with an access need may face additional barriers.

Look at the effort required, not just the number of channels offered.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**The difficult moment may sit between teams.**

Support might accept a request that fulfilment never receives. A smooth front end cannot solve a broken handoff by itself.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Map three steps in your chosen moment.

Reply:

- **My customer goal:** ___
- **Step 1:** They ___; need ___; possible difficulty ___.
- **Step 2:** They ___; need ___; possible difficulty ___.
- **Step 3:** They ___; need ___; possible difficulty ___.
- **The moment I want to improve:** ___

**Example:** Find change instructions → submit an eligible request → receive confirmation of the actual result.

You do not need to map an entire business. A small journey is enough. Describe unverified difficulties as possibilities.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Which step most affects your Day 1 need? Keep the connection. If you change your example, record why and carry the revised one forward.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a three-step journey and one place to investigate.

## Day 3 — Listen Before You Assume

👂 **DAY 3 OF 7 — FIND THE NEED BEHIND THE COMPLAINT**

You have mapped a difficult moment. Today, separate observation from interpretation.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. One useful question is progress.**

### 💭 “Your app is terrible.”

What happened?

Maybe the customer could not find the change instructions. Maybe the request failed. Maybe the app worked but a promise was unclear.

The complaint points to something worth exploring. It does not, by itself, tell us the cause.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** Feedback reaches a business through a conversation or letter.

**Today:** Feedback may arrive through reviews, surveys and support messages.

**Imagine 2050:** AI groups feedback into themes. People still check context, missing perspectives and whether the interpretation fits the evidence.

The question remains: **“Have we understood what happened before deciding what to fix?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“The customer is difficult.” → “What is making the task difficult?”**

Use three questions:

**1. GOAL:** What was the person trying to achieve?

**2. EXPERIENCE:** What happened at the difficult moment?

**3. EVIDENCE:** What do we know, what are we assuming and what should we ask?

### 🌍 People and impact

Some people leave without complaining. Others cannot easily use the feedback channel.

A handful of comments can reveal useful questions without representing every customer.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**“No complaints” does not prove “no problems.”**

People may abandon a task or accept an unsuitable workaround. Treat silence as missing evidence, not proof of success.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Use your Day 2 moment. Reply:

- **The customer's goal:** ___
- **One observation or fact:** ___
- **One assumption I am making:** ___
- **One open question I would ask:** ___
- **The need I now understand more clearly:** ___

**Example:** A fictional help page gives no address-change instructions. I assume customers would feel uncertain. I would ask: “What did you try when you realised the address was wrong?”

Use a fictional sample or your own non-sensitive experience. No interview is required. Do not copy private customer conversations into the challenge.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Has your original need changed? Keep the earlier wording and note the reason for the revision. Later, this helps explain why a requirement changed.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a clearer need and an honest account of what is still unknown.

## Day 4 — Write a Clear Experience Promise

📝 **DAY 4 OF 7 — YOUR FIRST CX IMPROVEMENT BRIEF**

Yesterday, you clarified the need. Today, turn it into a useful, observable improvement.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Plain language is enough.**

### 💭 “Make support better.”

A useful ambition—but what should change?

Try:

**“Help the customer understand whether an address change is still possible, what to do next and how the result will be confirmed.”**

A **requirement** describes what the solution or way of working must deliver.

An **acceptance check** explains how we will verify it.

An experience promise should reflect what the organisation can actually deliver.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** A service counter explains what can be changed and what cannot.

**Today:** The same promise needs to be understandable across relevant channels.

**Imagine 2050:** An assistant helps spot inconsistent wording and missing steps. People still agree the service rules, ownership and realistic commitment.

The question: **“Could someone understand the promise and check whether we kept it?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“Delight everyone.” → “Make a clear promise we can responsibly keep.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Plain instructions, meaningful labels and suitable alternatives can help people use a service. W3C explains that accessible digital design supports people with disabilities and can also help in other situations [2].

Consider access from the beginning.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**An honest limit can be part of a good experience.**

If an order can no longer be changed, a clear explanation and an appropriate next step are more useful than a false success message.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Complete this brief:

- **Customer need:** ___
- **Proposed improvement:** ___
- **Two must-haves:** ___ and ___
- **Who would need to agree or own it:** ___
- **How I would check it:** ___

**Example:** A customer needs to know whether an address change is possible. Improve the instructions. Must-haves: explain eligibility under the fictional order rules and show the next step for both eligible and ineligible cases. Service and fulfilment owners agree the wording. Check two sample scenarios against those rules.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Link each must-have to your original need. Your acceptance check should verify a requirement, not simply confirm that a page or message exists.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a clear improvement brief with a way to check it.

## Day 5 — Choose the Simplest Useful Improvement

⚖️ **DAY 5 OF 7 — COMPARE WAYS TO HELP**

You have a brief. Today, compare two ways to meet it.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. No software research needed.**

### 💭 Does this need a chatbot?

For our fictional address-change example:

**Option A:** Clear order-help instructions with an appropriate route to an accountable support person.

**Option B:** A guided self-service request that checks eligibility and confirms the result, with human help when needed.

Either could be useful. Which fits the customer need and operational reality?

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** A clear sign or helpful conversation changes the experience.

**Today:** Compare changes to information, staff support, process and technology.

**Imagine 2050:** AI suggests alternatives. People still ask whether each one is useful, inclusive and feasible.

The enduring question: **“What helps the customer without creating another problem?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“Add another feature.” → “Remove an unnecessary obstacle.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Self-service may help some people while others need assistance.

Consider staff effort too. A promise that creates an unmanageable support burden may be difficult to sustain.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**Personalisation is not always necessary.**

A clear answer that works for everyone eligible may be more useful than collecting more customer information.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Use your own ideas or the two fictional options above.

| Question | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| Does it meet the must-haves? | ___ | ___ |
| Can the intended customer use it? | ___ | ___ |
| Can the team deliver and support it? | ___ | ___ |
| What effort or cost is involved? | ___ | ___ |
| What remains unknown or risky? | ___ | ___ |

Use yes / no / unknown where helpful. The table is optional; a short explanation is enough.

Reply:

- **My two options:** ___ and ___
- **My preferred option:** ___
- **Two reasons linked to the need:** ___
- **One trade-off or assumption:** ___
- **One question before proceeding:** ___

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Which Day 4 requirement does your choice meet? Do not give a confident “yes” when the supporting evidence is still missing.

🎉 **Today's win:** You can explain an improvement choice through the customer need and delivery realities.

## Day 6 — Rehearse Before You Launch

🧪 **DAY 6 OF 7 — CHECK THE EXPERIENCE, NOT JUST THE SCREEN**

You chose an improvement. Today, try it against a realistic situation.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Use a fictional sample, not a live order.**

### 💭 “Your request has been received.”

Does that mean the address changed?

Not necessarily.

A request received, a request approved and a change completed are different states. The customer needs an accurate message about what has actually happened.

**Testing checks a solution against agreed requirements. Readiness checks whether the people, process and support are prepared for use.**

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** A team rehearses how to handle a request before using a new procedure.

**Today:** Check the journey across its messages, handoffs and actual result.

**Imagine 2050:** AI generates more scenarios. People still check whether they are relevant and verify the outcomes.

The question: **“Can the customer understand the result and take the next step?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“The button worked.” → “The customer received the intended, accurate result.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Try a situation beyond the easiest case: an ineligible request, an unavailable agent or instructions viewed on a small screen.

A quick rehearsal can reveal a barrier. It cannot prove the whole service is accessible or ready for every user.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**A clear failure message can be more trustworthy than a misleading success message.**

When a change cannot be completed, say what happened and provide an appropriate next step.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Create a three-line sample instruction or response for your improvement. Check one scenario.

Reply:

- **Requirement I am checking:** ___
- **Scenario:** ___
- **Expected result:** ___
- **What I checked or observed:** ___
- **Result:** Met / Not met / Not tested yet
- **One fix or readiness action:** ___

**Example:** For an already-dispatched fictional order, the message must not claim an address change succeeded. Check the sample wording and its next step against the fictional service rule.

If you only plan the check, write **“Not tested yet.”** Do not invent a result.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Link the scenario to a Day 4 requirement and the original need. Before a real launch, owners would review unresolved issues, staff guidance, support and approvals. One passed sample check is not complete go-live readiness.

🎉 **Today's win:** You can distinguish a working interaction from a checked customer outcome.

## Day 7 — Your Customer Experience Origin Story

🗺️ **DAY 7 OF 7 — CONNECT THE PROMISE TO THE RESULT**

You explored a customer need, mapped a journey, questioned assumptions, wrote requirements, compared improvements and planned or performed a check.

Today, connect the pieces.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Your own words are enough.**

### 💭 Look at your starting point again

Perhaps you first wanted to add a chatbot.

Now you see the need for clear eligibility instructions, accurate messages and an accountable route to help.

Or perhaps automation is useful—but only after the service rules and handoffs are clear.

Learning can change the solution.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** Help a customer complete a purchase or resolve a question.

**Today:** Connect the whole journey, including promises, channels and operations.

**Imagine 2050:** People and AI coordinate more service tasks. Customers still need meaningful choices and organisations still need accountability.

The future question: **“What kind of relationship are we helping create?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“We launched an improvement.” → “We can show what it was meant to help, what we checked and what we still need to learn.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Fewer support contacts could mean an easier journey—or that people gave up.

A completed task and a useful customer outcome are related, but not identical. Combine appropriate measures with context and feedback.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**A metric can improve while an experience gets worse.**

Closing requests faster does not prove customers received useful help. Ask what the measure includes and what it might miss.

### 🧵 Meet the traceability matrix

A **traceability matrix** connects:

**Origin Story → need → requirements → improvement → tests → readiness → outcomes.**

It helps us ask:

- During requirement gathering: “Which customer need does this serve?”
- During testing: “Which requirement does this scenario verify?”
- Before go-live: “What is ready, what is unresolved and who owns the decision?”
- After use: “What evidence suggests the intended benefit happened?”

Follow the links in either direction. When a need changes, review the connected requirements and checks.

## Coach-only appendix — From CX Origin Story to traceability

### Keep the introduction proportionate

Daily text submissions provide the starting evidence. Do not require beginners to maintain a formal enterprise matrix.

Later bootcamps can add stable IDs, stakeholders, owners, versions, evidence links, test results, defects, dependencies and approvals.

The Origin Story explains purpose and context. It does not replace stakeholder validation, service rules, clear requirements, research or appropriate governance.

### Fictional example: changing a delivery address

**OS-01 — Origin Story:** A customer discovers the address on an order is wrong and wants an accurate next step before dispatch.

**NEED-01 — Need to validate:** Customers can understand whether an address change is allowed, request it through an appropriate route and understand the actual result.

**Exercise assumptions:** In this fictional service, an eligible order can be changed before dispatch after appropriate identity checks. A dispatched order cannot use that route. These are teaching assumptions, not any real merchant's policy.

All records below are proposed and unverified. No real customer operation, test, launch or outcome has occurred.

| Origin / need | Requirement | Proposed decision / evidence | Acceptance scenario | Readiness consideration | Outcome evidence after use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-01: Instructions explain eligibility and next steps for eligible and ineligible cases | DEC-01: Approved help content aligned to agreed fictional rules | TEST-01: Review one pre-dispatch and one post-dispatch case against the rules | READY-01: Service and fulfilment owners agree content, rules and ownership | OUT-01: Feedback on whether customers understood their next step |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-02: The flow distinguishes receipt of a request from completion of an actual address change | DEC-02: Agreed state definitions and confirmation content | TEST-02: With synthetic data, verify that a received-only request is not shown as completed; check the saved result for a successful case | READY-02: State handling, exception ownership and support guidance reviewed | OUT-02: Review discrepancies between confirmation and actual fulfilment |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-03: The agreed flow uses appropriate identity checks and avoids exposing another customer's details | DEC-03: Approved identity, access and information-handling design | TEST-03: Positive and negative access scenarios using synthetic accounts | READY-03: Relevant reviewers sign off access controls and support procedure | OUT-03: Review appropriately logged access issues without unnecessary personal data |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-04: A suitable support route exists when self-service cannot complete the task | DEC-04: Clear handoff, accountable owner and agreed support commitment | TEST-04: Rehearse an exception and verify the next step and information passed | READY-04: Staff guidance, support capacity and escalation ownership confirmed | OUT-04: Review unresolved cases, repeat contacts and customer feedback in context |

Actual implementation needs more detailed requirements and tests, including relevant accessibility, security, integration, concurrency, failure and operational scenarios. This example is not exhaustive.

A change in order state during processing is a possible later test case: an eligibility check made earlier must not be assumed to remain valid indefinitely.

### What traceability helps reveal

- Requirements with no validated customer need.
- Tests with no relevant requirement, or requirements with no test.
- Assumptions presented as established evidence.
- Failed checks or unresolved issues affecting readiness.
- Changes in service rules affecting content, processes, integrations or tests.
- Successful technical checks without evidence of the intended customer benefit.

Traceability is bidirectional and can be many-to-many. An ID is a reference, not proof. Preserve actual evidence, owners, status and revision history.

Go-live readiness also involves operations, communication, support, security, accessibility and appropriate approvals. A complete matrix alone is not a launch decision.

### A simple outcome-measure plan

| Intended benefit | Possible evidence | Question before interpreting it |
|---|---|---|
| Customers understand eligibility | Optional task-based feedback | Who responded, and whose perspective is missing? |
| Requests reach an accurate result | Sample of synthetic test results, then appropriately governed operational evidence | Does “completed” match the actual saved and fulfilled state? |
| Customers expend less unnecessary effort | Repeat-contact patterns and feedback | Did contacts fall because the task became easier, or because help became harder to reach? |
| Support remains sustainable | Staff feedback, queue patterns and unresolved cases | Did the change simply move work to another team? |

Set baselines, definitions, measurement periods and owners before real evaluation. Do not invent targets or claim causation from a single number.

### Connection to the other Origin Story challenges

The shared pattern is:

**Purpose → need → requirements → decision → checks → readiness → outcome.**

- Procurement follows a buying decision and its consequences.
- HR follows an employee-related improvement and its intended human benefit.
- CX follows a customer goal, the service promise and what actually happens.

Use the same habits in Finance, SCM, Business Transformation Management, and Leadership & Identity Transformation, with appropriate evidence for each domain.
